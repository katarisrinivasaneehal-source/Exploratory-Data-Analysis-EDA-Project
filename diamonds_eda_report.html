The dataset contains price and physical/quality attributes for diamonds: carat (weight), cut, color (D=best to J=worst), clarity, depth and table (proportions), price, and physical dimensions x, y, z (mm).

Before analysis, 23 rows were removed for physically impossible values — diamonds recorded with a 0mm dimension, or one row where a dimension was listed as 58.9mm (clearly a data-entry error, likely meant to be 5.89mm). This is standard practice in EDA: check for entry errors before trusting summary statistics.

3. Distributions

  • Price is heavily right-skewed — most diamonds are cheaper, with a long tail of expensive outliers (few very large/rare stones).
  • Carat is also right-skewed, clustering around common "round number" weights (0.3, 0.5, 1.0 carat) — a sign that diamonds are often cut to hit marketable size thresholds.
  • Depth % and Table % are roughly normal, reflecting standardized cutting proportions.

4. Categorical Breakdown

Most diamonds in the dataset are graded Ideal or Premium cut, with color grades clustering in the middle range (F–H) and clarity most commonly SI1/VS2 — near-flawless (IF) and heavily-included (I1) stones are both rare.

5. Correlations & Key Relationships

Key influencing factor: Carat is overwhelmingly the strongest driver of price (correlation = 0.922). Physical size dimensions (x, y, z) are highly correlated with carat and each other, since they're all measuring the same underlying stone size. By contrast, Depth% (correlation = -0.011) and Table% (correlation = 0.127) — the cutting proportions — show almost no linear relationship with price on their own.

6. Price by Quality Grades

Surprising insight: Fair-cut diamonds have a higher average price ($4,358) than Ideal-cut diamonds ($3,457) — the opposite of what you'd expect if cut quality alone drove price. This happens because carat weight dominates price so strongly that it swamps the cut-quality effect: sellers who own large, low-cut-quality stones still charge more than sellers of small, perfectly-cut stones. This is a classic example of a confounding variable — cut and carat aren't independent in this market, so comparing price by cut alone is misleading without controlling for size.

7. Summary of Findings

  • Carat (size) is the single biggest price driver by far — far more influential than cut, color, or clarity individually.
  • Color and clarity have a real but secondary effect: within a similar carat range, better color/clarity grades command higher prices (visible in the boxplots once size is controlled for).
  • Cutting proportions (depth%, table%) barely affect price in this dataset — they matter more for visual brilliance than for market pricing.
  • Data quality matters: a small number of entry errors (0mm or 58.9mm dimensions) could have skewed correlation results if left in — always inspect before analyzing.
  • Confounding variables are a real risk: naive comparisons (e.g. "Fair cuts cost more than Ideal cuts") can be misleading without accounting for other factors like size.
